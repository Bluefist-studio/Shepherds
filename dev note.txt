# Sheep Herding Game — Prototype V0.1

## Overview

Create a playable prototype of a 2D top-down sheep-herding game using:

* HTML5
* CSS
* Vanilla JavaScript
* HTML5 Canvas
* No external game engine
* No external libraries unless absolutely necessary

The project will be developed in Visual Studio.

The player controls a sheepdog and must use the dog's movement, positioning, and barking to influence a flock of autonomous sheep and bring the entire flock into a designated target zone.

The game is intentionally abstract at this stage. The dog and sheep should be represented as simple colored shapes/blobs. Do not spend time creating detailed art or animations.

The priority is making the **herding simulation and controls feel good**.

---

# 1. Core Gameplay

The player controls one sheepdog.

A level contains:

* A playable environment
* Obstacles
* A flock of sheep
* A target zone

The objective is:

> Get the entire flock into the target zone.

The level is completed when all required sheep are inside the target zone.

The sheep must remain autonomous. The player does NOT directly control individual sheep.

The player controls only the dog.

The fundamental gameplay loop should be:

1. Position the dog.
2. Apply pressure to the sheep through proximity and barking.
3. Sheep react according to their individual behavior.
4. Reposition the dog.
5. Gather and direct the flock.
6. Bring the flock into the target zone.

---

# 2. Visual Style

Use a simple abstract visual style.

### Dog

Represent the dog as a clearly identifiable blob/circle.

The dog should have an indication of its facing/movement direction.

### Sheep

Represent sheep as simple circles/blobs.

Use colors to represent their behavioral types:

* Green = normal
* Yellow = nervous
* Orange = independent
* Red = nervous + independent

The colors are primarily a development/debugging tool at this stage.

### Environment

Use simple geometric shapes for:

* Grass/background
* Fences
* Walls
* Obstacles
* Target zone

Do not create detailed artwork.

---

# 3. Dog Controls

Use WASD for dog movement.

* W = move up
* S = move down
* A = move left
* D = move right

Movement must use acceleration and deceleration rather than instantly reaching maximum speed.

### Movement modes

**Jog**

* Default movement speed.

**Walk**

* Hold C or Ctrl.
* Dog moves at walking speed.

**Sprint**

* Hold Shift.
* Dog moves at sprint speed.
* Sprint consumes stamina.

The movement system should feel smooth and responsive.

Do not use instant velocity changes.

---

# 4. Dog Stats

Create a centralized configuration system so these values can easily be modified without searching through the code.

Initial dog properties:

### Movement

* walkSpeed
* jogSpeed
* sprintSpeed
* acceleration
* deceleration
* turnRate/agility

The exact initial values should be chosen to make the prototype playable, but all values must be easily adjustable.

### Stamina

* maxStamina
* sprintStaminaDrain
* staminaRecoveryRate

Stamina decreases while sprinting.

Stamina recovers while walking, jogging, or standing still.

The bark also consumes stamina.

Do not create a separate bark cooldown system at this stage.

### Presence

The dog has a `presenceStrength` stat.

The dog's physical proximity to sheep creates pressure.

Presence should be stronger when the dog is closer to a sheep and weaker when farther away.

The exact mathematical falloff should be implemented as a configurable system.

### Bark

The dog has:

* barkStrength
* barkRange
* barkStaminaCost

When the player activates the bark, sheep within the bark range receive a burst of pressure.

The bark should increase sheep nervousness and influence their movement.

The bark consumes stamina.

Use a simple key for barking, such as Space.

Make the bark visually obvious during development with a temporary expanding circle/ring around the dog.

---

# 5. Camera

Use a top-down camera.

The camera should NOT simply remain centered on the dog.

Instead:

> The mouse controls where the player looks.

The desired camera position should move toward the mouse cursor.

However, the camera must remain within a maximum radius around the dog.

Conceptually:

```text
             Camera
                *
              / |
             /  |
            /   |
           /    |
        🐕 Dog
```

The camera can look ahead of the dog, but cannot move beyond the configured maximum radius from the dog.

Create configurable values for:

* cameraLookRadius
* cameraSmoothing
* mouseSensitivity

Use smooth camera movement.

The dog should therefore be able to move independently of where the player is looking.

---

# 6. Sheep Simulation

Each sheep is an autonomous entity.

The player does not directly issue movement commands to sheep.

Sheep movement should emerge from several behavioral forces.

At minimum, sheep should consider:

1. Dog avoidance
2. Flock attraction
3. Sheep-to-sheep separation
4. Social preferences
5. Grazing/wandering
6. Obstacles
7. Nervousness

These forces should be combined to determine the sheep's desired movement.

Do not hard-code individual scripted movements.

---

# 7. Sheep Nervousness

Every sheep has a continuously changing nervousness value.

Use a normalized range such as:

```text
0.0 = completely calm
1.0 = extremely nervous
```

Nervousness should:

* Increase from dog presence.
* Increase from barking.
* Recover when the dog is no longer applying significant pressure.

Nervousness must be a dynamic state, not a permanent sheep property.

Create configurable values for:

* nervousnessGain
* nervousnessRecovery
* nervousnessMovementMultiplier
* breakawayThreshold

Higher nervousness should cause:

* Faster movement.
* Stronger flight response.
* Greater tendency to break away from the flock.

Do not make nervousness immediately snap between states. It should change continuously.

---

# 8. Sheep Types

Implement four sheep archetypes.

## Green Sheep — Normal

Green sheep are the baseline sheep.

Behavior:

* Strong tendency to stay with the flock.
* Normal nervousness gain.
* Normal movement speed.
* Naturally wander/spread slightly while grazing.
* Do not remain perfectly stationary.
* When not being influenced by the dog, the flock should slowly spread and change shape.
* Can break away when sufficiently pressured.

Green sheep should be the easiest sheep to herd.

---

## Yellow Sheep — Nervous

Yellow sheep are more sensitive.

Behavior:

* Gain nervousness more quickly.
* Become nervous from less dog pressure.
* Move faster when nervous.
* More likely to break away from the flock.
* Should be more difficult to control once highly nervous.
* Should recover nervousness more slowly than green sheep if appropriate.

Yellow sheep should create situations where the player needs to be careful about approaching too aggressively.

---

## Orange Sheep — Independent

Orange sheep have different social behavior rather than simply being "more nervous."

Orange sheep:

* Still want to remain associated with the flock.
* Do NOT want to stay close to other orange or red sheep.
* Do not inherently avoid green or yellow sheep.
* Should naturally distribute themselves among the other sheep.
* Have normal nervousness unless otherwise affected.

Important:

Orange sheep should NOT have a large generic personal-space radius around every sheep.

Their special avoidance applies specifically to:

* Orange sheep
* Red sheep

They can comfortably stay close to green and yellow sheep.

---

## Red Sheep — Nervous + Independent

Red sheep combine the yellow and orange behaviors.

They:

* Gain nervousness quickly.
* Move faster when nervous.
* Are more likely to break away.
* Want to remain associated with the flock.
* Avoid being too close to orange and red sheep.
* Do not inherently avoid green or yellow sheep.

Red sheep should be among the most difficult sheep to manage.

---

# 9. Sheep Social Behavior

The sheep should not simply form one rigid ball.

The flock should constantly change shape.

There should be a balance between:

### Flock attraction

Sheep want to remain near the flock.

### Separation

Sheep avoid physically overlapping or occupying the exact same space.

### Social avoidance

Orange and red sheep specifically avoid being close to other orange/red sheep.

### Grazing

When there is little or no dog pressure, sheep should wander slightly and spread out.

This should create a natural "breathing" flock.

The flock should look alive even when the player is doing nothing.

---

# 10. Sheep Physical Interaction

Sheep should have mass.

Sheep can push against one another.

Avoid implementing sheep as ghosts that simply pass through each other.

At minimum, implement circle-based collision/repulsion so sheep cannot occupy the same physical space.

Mass should influence how strongly sheep push one another.

Keep this system simple for the first prototype.

Do not attempt a fully realistic physics simulation.

---

# 11. Dog Influence

The dog should influence sheep primarily through physical positioning.

Basic behavior:

> Dog approaches sheep → sheep move away from dog.

The closer the dog is, the stronger the pressure should generally be.

Dog presence should not simply teleport or directly command sheep movement.

Instead, it should contribute a force/desired movement direction.

The sheep's other behaviors should interact with this pressure.

For example:

* Dog pressure pushes sheep away.
* Flock attraction pulls sheep toward other sheep.
* Social avoidance separates orange/red sheep.
* Grazing creates small wandering movement.
* Nervousness modifies the strength and speed of their response.

The resulting movement should emerge from the combination of these behaviors.

---

# 12. Bark

When the player presses Space:

* The dog barks.
* A visual bark indicator appears.
* Sheep inside the bark range receive additional pressure.
* Their nervousness increases.
* Their movement response becomes stronger.
* Stamina is consumed.

The bark should not directly tell sheep which direction to move.

It should simply apply additional pressure from the dog's location.

Barking should therefore be useful for getting sheep moving but potentially dangerous if overused.

---

# 13. Obstacles

Create basic static obstacles.

For the first prototype, use simple rectangles and/or polygons.

Sheep and dog should not be able to move through solid obstacles.

Sheep should react to obstacles rather than becoming permanently stuck whenever possible.

Include at least:

* Open field
* One fence/wall
* One narrow passage or gate-like opening

Obstacles are important because the goal is eventually to create levels where the player must figure out how to position the dog around environmental constraints.

---

# 14. Target Zone

Create a clearly visible target zone.

The target zone should be larger than an individual sheep.

The level completes when all required sheep are inside the target zone simultaneously.

Display a simple success message when the level is completed.

---

# 15. Initial Prototype Level

Create one test level.

The level should contain:

* One dog
* Approximately 15–20 sheep
* A mixture of green, yellow, orange, and red sheep
* An open field
* A few obstacles
* A target zone positioned away from the starting flock

Use a deliberately mixed flock so all four sheep behaviors can be tested.

The initial flock composition should be configurable.

---

# 16. Debugging / Development UI

Because this is a prototype, include a simple debug overlay.

Display:

* Dog speed
* Current stamina
* Maximum stamina
* Number of sheep
* Number of sheep currently in target zone
* Average flock nervousness

Optionally allow debug visualization of:

* Dog presence radius
* Bark radius
* Sheep nervousness
* Sheep desired movement direction
* Flock center

The debug UI should make it easy to understand why the sheep are behaving a certain way.

---

# 17. Code Architecture

Keep the code modular.

Do not create one enormous JavaScript file containing the entire game.

Separate major systems where practical.

Suggested structure:

```text
sheep-herding/
│
├── index.html
├── css/
│   └── style.css
│
├── js/
│   ├── main.js
│   ├── game.js
│   ├── dog.js
│   ├── sheep.js
│   ├── flock.js
│   ├── camera.js
│   ├── input.js
│   ├── level.js
│   ├── physics.js
│   └── config.js
│
└── assets/
```

Do not create unnecessary complexity just for the sake of having many files.

The important requirement is that systems remain logically separated.

# 18. Configuration

All important gameplay parameters must be centralized and easily adjustable.

Do not bury important numbers throughout the code.

The prototype should allow easy adjustment of:

* Dog movement speeds
* Acceleration
* Deceleration
* Turn rate
* Stamina
* Stamina drain
* Stamina recovery
* Presence strength
* Bark strength
* Bark range
* Bark stamina cost
* Sheep movement speed
* Sheep mass
* Flock attraction
* Separation strength
* Social avoidance
* Nervousness gain
* Nervousness recovery
* Nervousness movement effect
* Breakaway threshold
* Camera radius
* Camera smoothing

---

# 19. Performance

The game may eventually contain significantly larger flocks.

Build the sheep simulation with reasonable performance in mind.

Avoid unnecessarily expensive calculations.

For the initial prototype, approximately 15–20 sheep is sufficient, but the architecture should not assume that the game will always have only 20 sheep.

Do not prematurely optimize at the expense of readable code.

---

# 20. Future Systems — DO NOT IMPLEMENT YET

Design the architecture so these could be added later, but do not implement them in this prototype:

* Dog leveling
* Dog experience
* Money
* Purchasing dogs
* Different dog breeds
* Dog training
* Dog food
* Dog accessories
* Multiple dogs
* Campaign progression
* Advanced missions
* Detailed dog animations
* Detailed sheep models
* Sound system
* Multiplayer

The current goal is purely to establish whether the **core sheep-herding simulation is fun**.

# 21. Development Priority

Build the game in this order:

### Phase 1

Create Canvas, game loop, input, dog movement, and camera.

### Phase 2

Add basic sheep entities and movement.

### Phase 3

Add flock attraction, separation, and grazing behavior.

### Phase 4

Add dog presence and sheep avoidance.

### Phase 5

Add nervousness.

### Phase 6

Add the four sheep archetypes.

### Phase 7

Add barking and stamina interaction.

### Phase 8

Add obstacles and collision.

### Phase 9

Add target zone and level completion.

### Phase 10

Add debugging tools and tune the simulation.

At every phase, keep the game playable.

Do not attempt to implement the entire game at once and then debug everything simultaneously.

---

# 22. Most Important Design Principle

The game should feel like the player is **herding animals**, not controlling units.

The player should need to learn:

* Where to position the dog.
* How close to approach.
* When to back off.
* When to sprint around the flock.
* When to bark.
* How to handle nervous sheep.
* How to prevent sheep from breaking away.
* How to use obstacles and terrain.

The sheep should behave autonomously enough that the player must respond to them rather than simply issuing commands.

Prioritize **emergent flock behavior and satisfying dog/sheep interaction** over graphics, menus, progression, or polish.
